Overview

India — The Forgotten Heart of the Ancient World

Type "Silk Road" into any search engine and you will be met with thousands of results, dozens of documentaries, and an entire popular mythology centred on the overland route connecting China to the Mediterranean. Type "India's role in the ancient world" and the results are comparatively sparse. That asymmetry — in popular memory, in publishing, in pedagogy — is precisely what William Dalrymple sets out to correct in The Golden Road, his most ambitious book to date.

Dalrymple's central argument is both simple and startling. From roughly 250 BCE to 1200 CE — a span of nearly fifteen centuries — India was the gravitational centre of Eurasian civilisation. Not through military conquest, not through imperial subjugation, but through what we might today call soft power: the irresistible allure of its ideas, its art, its mathematics, its philosophies, and its merchants. The route these ideas travelled — primarily maritime, riding the monsoon winds that Indian sailors had mapped and mastered centuries before anyone else — he names for the first time: the Golden Road.

This is, as Dalrymple freely acknowledges, a familiar claim to Indian scholars and to anyone acquainted with the literature. What is new is the synthesis: never before has this story been marshalled across its full geographic and temporal sweep, from the Red Sea port of Berenike to the Tang dynasty court of Chang'an, from the Ajanta caves to the Khmer temples of Cambodia, and told with the narrative verve for which Dalrymple has no peer among popular historians of South Asia.

Everywhere I could see India, yet I could not recognize it.

— Rabindranath Tagore, on his 1927 visit to Southeast Asia, quoted by Dalrymple

Tagore's observation — bewildered wonder at an India he recognised but could not place — captures perfectly what The Golden Road aims to restore: a self-understanding for readers who have been educated to think of India as the perpetual recipient of outside influence rather than its greatest ancient donor.

The Author

A Lifetime in the Subcontinent

William Dalrymple is, by any measure, the most widely read historian of South Asia writing in English today. A Scottish writer who has made India his home for three decades, he is the co-founder of the Jaipur Literature Festival, a Fellow of the Royal Society of Literature and of the Royal Asiatic Society, and the author of a body of work that has consistently brought rigorous scholarship to a mass readership.

His earlier books traced the more recent centuries of South Asian history — White Mughals examined the cultural fusion of the Mughal court and British officers; The Last Mughal reconstructed the fall of Delhi in 1857; Return of a King narrated the catastrophe of the First Anglo-Afghan War; and The Anarchy — perhaps his most celebrated work — told the staggering story of the East India Company's corporate conquest of a subcontinent. These are books about encounter, about conquest, about the end of things.

The Golden Road — the prequel to everything

His most chronologically ambitious work yet: a history of India not as a land conquered but as a civilisation that conquered the world through ideas, trade, and culture.

The Golden Road is, in a sense, the prequel to all of these — the story of what India was before the disasters began. It is a book about power held gently, about influence exercised through attraction rather than force, about a civilisation so confident in its own worth that it had no need to name its own empire. It required, Dalrymple writes, "a lifetime of scholarship" to accumulate the material needed to tell it.

The Western Connection

India and Rome — Forgotten Partners

The book opens in early March 2022, at Berenike — a desolate stretch of the Egyptian Red Sea coast that turns out to be one of the most revelatory archaeological sites of the century. From a storeroom of the Isis temple, a team excavating the site dislodges the head and torso of a Buddha, sculpted from Proconnesian marble and almost certainly fabricated in a workshop in second-century Alexandria — the first Buddhist image ever discovered to the west of Afghanistan. Alongside it: pottery from Spain, beads from Vietnam and Java, and enormous quantities of Indian black peppercorns.

This discovery encapsulates the book's opening argument: that India, not China, was Rome's greatest trading partner. The first trade routes between India and Europe were not overland but maritime — riding the monsoon winds that Indian sailors had understood and exploited for centuries, ships could carry cargoes no caravan could match, sailing around wars, blockades, and mountain passes with ease.

The Trade Deficit That May Have Broken an Empire

The scale of Rome-India trade was staggering. Dalrymple estimates the value of Indian goods — diamonds, rubies, silks, sandalwood, ebony, teak, ivory, pepper, and exotic animals for the Coliseum's spectacles — at over one billion sesterces entering Roman ports each year. Roman senators complained repeatedly about the gold haemorrhaging east.

Roman gold flowed into India in such quantities that some economic historians have speculated the trade imbalance may have been a factor in the Empire's eventual fiscal collapse — a more material explanation for Rome's fall than the Christianity that Gibbon famously blamed. India, meanwhile, used this influx to fund an extraordinary efflorescence of art, architecture, and scholarship.

The city of Mamallapuram, on the Coromandel coast of what is now Tamil Nadu, stands as the physical monument to this wealth. In the heyday of the Pallava dynasty, from around 600 to 900 CE, it was arguably the greatest seaport in India. The astonishing sculptures carved directly from the coastal rocks — gods and goddesses, lions, elephants, cobras, water buffalo — were the calling cards of a civilisation that knew it was being watched and wanted to impress. The ships that once rode at anchor off its shore connected it to the known world.

The Spiritual Superpower

Buddhism — India's Greatest Export

If commerce was one vehicle for India's influence, religion was the other — and of India's religious exports, none travelled further or transformed more utterly than Buddhism. Born in the foothills of the Himalayas, carried along maritime routes and mountain passes, Buddhism became the dominant faith of China, Japan, Korea, Tibet, Southeast Asia, and much of Central Asia. Dalrymple devotes the book's longest and richest section to this extraordinary journey.

Dalrymple's account of the monk Xuanzang is among the most vivid in the book. In 629 CE, in turbulent times, this Chinese scholar defied the direct orders of Emperor Taizong to undertake a three-thousand-mile journey across the Taklamakan desert, through the passes of Central Asia, and into the Indian subcontinent — driven by a hunger for the source texts of Buddhist philosophy and a need to learn at the feet of its greatest living masters.

No freshman from the sticks can ever have had his mind more thoroughly blown by the university experience.

— Ferdinand Mount, London Review of Books, on Xuanzang's arrival at Nalanda

What Xuanzang found at Nalanda — endless courtyards and temples, ten thousand monks and scholars engaged in philosophy, mathematics, logic, medicine, and metaphysics — had no equivalent anywhere in the world. The university system that would eventually produce Oxford, Cambridge, and the Sorbonne traces its lineage, Dalrymple argues, through the Buddhist monasteries of northern India, through the madrasas of the Islamic world, to that astonishing institution in Bihar. The idea of gathering scholars in a secluded community dedicated to learning is, at its root, an Indian idea.

Cultural Geography

The Indosphere — An Empire Without Emperors

The concept around which the book's second half organises itself is the "Indosphere" — Dalrymple's term for the vast cultural zone shaped by Indian religions, languages, arts, and ideas that stretched from Afghanistan and the Malabar coast eastward to Vietnam, Cambodia, Java, and beyond. It is one of the most consequential cultural formations in human history, and one of the least recognised.

What the Indosphere Looked Like

Sanskrit inscriptions in the jungles of Indonesia. Hindu temples in Vietnam that rival those in Tamil Nadu itself. Buddhist monasteries in Japan modelled on Indian originals. The Mahabharata and Ramayana performed as living epics in Bali and Thailand. Khmer kings adopting Sanskrit as their court language and Hindu cosmology as their state theology. Indian astronomical tables at the court of the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad. Indian mathematical notation in the libraries of Muslim Andalusia.

This was not an empire imposed by force. It was a cultural universe adopted by choice — by rulers who found in Indian civilisation an incomparable source of prestige, sophistication, and metaphysical authority. The Khmer king who built Angkor Wat was not Indian. He was Cambodian, and he chose to make the largest religious monument on earth a temple to the Hindu cosmos.

Angkor Wat Borobudur Srivijaya Chola Dynasty Pallava Sanskrit Cosmopolis

With the decline of Roman trade in the fifth century, Indian merchants pivoted east. The resulting sea trade with China through the Java Sea and the Malacca Straits gave rise, along its navigation routes, to the great Indianised polities of Southeast Asia: Srivijaya in Sumatra, the Sailendra dynasty in Java (builders of Borobudur, the world's largest Buddhist monument), and the Khmer empire in Cambodia, whose Angkor Wat is four times the size of the Vatican City.

The Golden Road is a vivid and richly detailed story. Dalrymple is a born storyteller, with a wonderful facility for expounding complex events with verve and clarity.

What distinguishes Dalrymple's account from earlier treatments of "Greater India" — a concept that generated controversy in the 1920s and 1930s for its presumption that Southeast Asian cultures were passive recipients of Indian civilisation — is his consistent insistence on indigenous agency. The Khmer rulers, the Srivijayan kings, the Javanese court poets: they were not Indianised against their will. They actively sought out and adopted Indian models because they found in them an intellectual and aesthetic universe of extraordinary richness and prestige. Culture flows toward beauty. India, in this period, was the most beautiful civilisation the world had yet produced.

The Intellectual Legacy

Zero, Algebra, and the Numbers We Use Today

Among the most consequential sections of the book — and the one most likely to genuinely surprise general readers — is Dalrymple's account of India's mathematical contributions to the world. The numerals used in every country on earth, in every currency, in every scientific formula and computer programme, are called "Arabic numerals." They are Indian.

01

The Invention of Zero

The concept of zero as a number — not merely a placeholder but a quantity in its own right, capable of operations — was developed by Indian mathematicians, notably Brahmagupta in the seventh century. No other civilisation independently produced this idea. Without it, modern mathematics, physics, computing, and finance are impossible.

02

Base-Ten Numerals

The nine figures of the Indian system were transmitted to the Arab world, refined, and eventually carried into medieval Europe by Fibonacci, whose Liber Abaci (1202) explicitly credited "the nine figures of the Indias." Dalrymple argues this discovery seeded the commercial revolution that financed the Renaissance and the economic rise of Europe.

03

Astronomy and Algebra

Indian astronomical tables were translated at the court of the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad, becoming the foundation of medieval Islamic science and, through it, of European astronomy. The word "algebra" derives from the Arabic title of a work building directly on Indian mathematical traditions.

04

The University System

The institutional model of a residential scholarly community — scholars housed, fed, and dedicated to learning — was pioneered at the great Buddhist monastic universities of India: Nalanda, Taxila, Vikramashila. This model passed through the Islamic madrasa into the medieval European university. Nalanda predates Oxford by six centuries.

05

Chess, Medicine, and Material Culture

Chess originated in India as chaturanga before travelling to Persia and then to the world. Indian medical knowledge, transmitted through Arabic translations, shaped medieval European medicine. Indian steel, textiles, spices, and precious stones defined the material culture of Rome at its height.

The mechanism of transmission is one of the book's most fascinating threads. Indian mathematical and astronomical knowledge did not travel west directly. It passed through Arab scholars at the Abbasid court in Baghdad, who absorbed, translated, and built upon it, before it reached medieval Europe via the Arab libraries of Muslim Spain. By the time it arrived, the Indian origin had been partially obscured — hence "Arabic numerals." Dalrymple is at pains to follow the full chain of transmission, giving credit where it has for too long been withheld.

It was this discovery of Indian mathematics alongside Indian accounting methods by Mediterranean Europe in the early thirteenth century that was responsible for seeding the commercial revolution that financed the Renaissance and the economic rise of Europe.

— William Dalrymple, The Golden Road
Critical Assessment

A Magnificent Achievement — With Caveats

The Golden Road is, by any measure, a magnificent achievement. It is one of those rare works of popular history that genuinely changes how you see the world — not by revealing entirely new facts (many of these stories are known to specialists) but by assembling them into a single narrative with enough sweep and authority to make the argument stick at last.

Dalrymple is a born storyteller. He has an enviable facility for the telling detail — the Buddha found in the Egyptian desert, the Roman senators complaining about gold flowing east, the Chinese monk weeping at the entrance to Nalanda — that makes abstract claims about civilisational influence concrete and human. His prose is vivid, warm, and lightly worn; never pedantic, never condescending, always propulsive.

What the Critics Said

The book was widely praised on publication, but not without reservations. Some reviewers — including Daud Ali in the Times Literary Supplement — noted that the concept of India's cultural "empire" to the east has a complex historiographical history, touching on earlier and now-discredited "Greater India" frameworks that portrayed Southeast Asian societies as passive recipients of Indian civilisation.

A more pointed critique came from scholars who noted that Dalrymple's account of Indian intellectual achievement is largely an account of upper-caste, Brahminical knowledge production — mathematicians like Aryabhata and Brahmagupta, philosopher-monks at elite monastic universities — while the contributions of artisans, farmers, and lower-caste practitioners go largely unexamined. This is a real limitation, though it is also a limitation shared by virtually all available sources from the period.

These caveats are worth registering. But they should not obscure the book's essential service. At a moment when India is the world's most populous nation and its fifth-largest economy, when it sits at the intersection of enormous geopolitical currents, the poverty of popular historical understanding of its precolonial significance is a genuine problem — for India's own self-understanding, and for the world's understanding of what Asian civilisations have contributed to the common human inheritance.

The Golden Road does not solve that problem, but it makes a powerful start. It restores to the general reader a story that colonial historiography systematically suppressed — a story of India not as a land of poverty and chaos awaiting European rescue, but as a place that, for a millennium and a half, gave the world its numerals, its university model, its greatest philosophical systems, and architectural wonders that dwarf anything the contemporary European world produced.

The Golden Road is a magnificent achievement. It is informative, stunningly comprehensive, and its stories are lavishly and compellingly told. It should help us recognize India in places where we could not before.

— Post and Courier, on The Golden Road

Tagore, visiting Southeast Asia in 1927, saw India everywhere but could not recognise it. Readers of this book, visiting the world after Dalrymple, will find they recognise India everywhere they look. In the numerals on their phone screen. In the zero at the centre of all mathematics. In the structure of every university. In the calm half-smile on the face of every Buddha, from Tokyo to Tehran. That recognition — long overdue — is The Golden Road's gift.
