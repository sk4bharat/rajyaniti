Overview

A Civilisational Reckoning

When Article 1 of India's Constitution opens with the words "India, that is Bharat…", it encodes within a single comma an unresolved tension — between a modern republic built on inherited European frameworks and an ancient civilisation reaching back millennia. Advocate and scholar J. Sai Deepak has written a book that takes that comma seriously.

India, that is Bharat is the first volume of a planned trilogy examining Bharat as a civilisation, its encounter with European colonial modernity, and the consequences that linger in its constitutional and legal architecture. The book spans the long arc from the Age of Discovery and the birth of colonialism to the Government of India Act of 1919 — the framework that, Sai Deepak argues, directly gave shape to independent India's Constitution of 1950.

Section I
Coloniality

Unpacks the distinction between colonisation as a historical event and coloniality as an enduring structure of knowledge, power, and being that survives independence.

Section II
Civilisation

Examines Bharat as a living civilisational entity with its own epistemology, ontology, and cosmology — and how colonial consciousness dismantled indigenous self-perception.

Section III
Constitution

Traces the direct lineage from British colonial governance structures to post-independence constitutional arrangements, showing what was — and was not — truly decolonised.

Praised by scholars from Duke University, Queen Mary London, Monash University, and the London School of Economics, the book has been called "a milestone" in the global decolonial literature — and particularly significant for filling a long-standing vacuum: Asia, and India in particular, had been conspicuously absent from decolonial scholarship largely concentrated in Latin American and African intellectual traditions.

The Author

The Unlikely Architect of an Argument

J. Sai Deepak's journey to this book reads like a bildungsroman of civilisational awakening. Growing up in a middle-class Hindu family in southern Bharat in the 1990s, he set out to study aerospace engineering — a thoroughly modern aspiration. By his sixth semester, however, his reading of Swami Vivekananda, Dr. Arun Shourie, and his visits to Ramakrishna Math had begun to unsettle the trajectory he had mapped for himself.

He describes noticing, with increasing clarity, a "normalised and open hostility" directed at specific Indic sub-identities on academic campuses — a hostility with, as he came to understand it, extra-civilisational sources and motivations. This recognition impelled a switch to law, and in 2006 he joined the Rajiv Gandhi School of Intellectual Property Law at IIT Kharagpur, one of the country's first IP-specialised law programmes open to engineering graduates.

The Sabarimala case proved pivotal. Standing before the Supreme Court and having to justify the legitimacy of indigenous Dharmic religious practice in a framework whose epistemological assumptions were themselves colonial products gave Sai Deepak, as he describes it, "the push I needed to understand the journey of the Indic civilisation better." From that dissonance grew the trilogy.

Section I

Coloniality — The Problem That Outlived the Empire

The book's most foundational intellectual move is to separate colonialism — the historical, territorial, political subjugation of one people by another — from coloniality: the matrix of power, knowledge, and being that colonialism produced and that persists long after the political relationship ends. This distinction, drawn from the Latin American decolonial tradition associated with scholars like Aníbal Quijano, Walter Mignolo, and Nelson Maldonado-Torres, forms the bedrock of the entire argument.

Decoloniality doesn't propose a return to a pristine past, but the reconstitution and restitution in the present — and towards the future — of destitutions carried out in the name of modernity, rationality, and emancipated thinking.

— Walter D. Mignolo, Duke University, from the book's endorsements

Sai Deepak traces the origins of coloniality not to the Enlightenment — as much postcolonial theory does — but to 1492 and the Age of Discovery, grounding it in its explicitly Christian theological rationalisation. This is significant: it means that "secularism," far from being a neutral check on colonial religious violence, is itself a product of Christian theological traditions, transposed into a supposedly universal language of governance.

The Coloniality/Modernity Matrix

Coloniality, in the framework Sai Deepak adapts for Bharat's context, operates across three registers simultaneously. The coloniality of power reorganises political and economic relations. The coloniality of knowledge — or epistemology — delegitimises indigenous ways of knowing. The coloniality of being produces ontological violence: the colonised subject internalises the coloniser's framework to understand even themselves.

For Bharat, this manifests in what Sai Deepak describes as a deeply internalised "colonial consciousness" — visible in judicial decisions, in the State's differential treatment of Hindu religious institutions, in the casual dismissal of precolonial history, and in the social elite's reflexive alignment with "extra-civilisational" frameworks of legitimacy.

A particularly searching argument concerns the nature of the East India Company's project. Against the dominant historiography that frames it as primarily commercial, Sai Deepak shows through original charters and declarations that the propagation of the Gospel was an avowed purpose from 1614 onwards, and that the Company was explicitly authorised to make war on "heathen nations" from 1683. This reframes the entire colonial encounter as, at its root, a civilisational and theological project rather than merely an economic one.

Section II

Bharat as Civilisation — Recovering a Self-Understanding

If Section I diagnoses the disease, Section II undertakes the work of recovery — reconstructing a framework for understanding Bharat not as a colonial administrative unit but as a living civilisation with its own distinct ontology, epistemology, and cosmology, one that the colonial project systematically dismantled and derided.

Sai Deepak draws on a wide range of Indic scholars — from Pandurang Vaman Kane and Jadunath Sarkar to Ram Swarup, Sitaram Goel, and Dharampal — as well as European scholars sympathetic to the Indic tradition, such as Koenraad Elst and Michel Danino. The picture that emerges is of a civilisational inheritance of extraordinary depth and internal diversity that colonial epistemology reduced to "Hinduism" — a category it then defined, bounded, and adjudicated from the outside.

01

The Construction of "Hindu Religion"

Colonial administrators imposed a Christian paradigm of religion — bounded doctrinal belief, institutionalised clergy, scriptural authority — onto Dharmic traditions that were structurally incommensurable with such categories. "Popular practices" were separated from a supposedly tolerable scriptural core and labelled superstition for elimination.

02

Temple as Civilisational Institution

The Hindu temple was not merely a place of worship but the economic, cultural, and educational centre of Indic social life. Its progressive subjugation through temple entry legislation — culminating in today's State control of Hindu religious endowments — represents a continuing colonial imposition that no comparable Abrahamic institution suffers in independent Bharat.

03

Caste, Tribe, and Colonial Sociology

The colonial census and administration crystallised and hardened fluid social categories into fixed identities amenable to administrative control and political manipulation. The resulting framework — still operational in independent India's reservation policies and electoral arithmetic — is a colonial construction, not an indigenous one.

04

Language, Education, and Epistemic Displacement

Macaulay's infamous minute on education is just the most celebrated instance of a systematic effort to produce a class "Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect." The resulting displacement of indigenous intellectual traditions from the sites of educational formation continues to shape the post-independence intelligentsia.

The book's treatment of the Indic relationship with nature — in contrast to the Cartesian subject-object duality that underpins European modernity and its extractive economics — is one of its most philosophically rich passages. Sai Deepak argues that Christian colonial ideology severed indigenous societies from a "seamless continuity of life with nature" that their Dharmic cosmologies had maintained, and that this rupture is inseparable from ecological crisis.

European coloniality is like the matrix — one just needs to become aware of it, after which it is impossible to unsee it, especially in matters of religion, polity, education, economics and the law.

— J. Sai Deepak, India that is Bharat
Section III

The Constitution and Its Colonial Architecture

The book's third and culminating movement is its most legally precise. Here Sai Deepak the Supreme Court advocate is most fully on display, working through the legislative and constitutional history with the forensic attention of someone who has lived inside these texts.

The central argument is that India's Constitution of 1950 was not built on a clean slate. The Government of India Act of 1919 — examined in detail — provided the framework of governance, while the Government of India Act of 1935 provided its architecture. The formal transfer of power in 1947–1950 transferred the exercise of authority while leaving largely intact a legal framework designed by the colonial sovereign to govern a subjugated civilisation.

Advocate J. Sai Deepak has provided India with a milestone: a step from superficial to integral decolonisation. The coloniser transferred the exercise of power in 1947–1950 but left in place a legal framework that continues to guide the lives of Indians in considerable detail.

Dr. Koenraad Elst Scholar & Author, Decolonizing the Hindu Mind

In a masterful manner, Sai Deepak traces the global history of colonialism, India's tryst with it, and enquires into its impact on colonial consciousness — combining the skills of an adept lawyer and a tenacious researcher.

Dr. Vikram Sampath Historian, Adjunct Senior Research Fellow, Monash University

In particular, Sai Deepak problematises the version of secularism that actually managed to worsen the imposition of an extraneous model on India's management of plurality.

The chapters on the 1919 Act and the Paris Peace Conference of the same year reveal something the broader public conversation rarely acknowledges: that the very notion of "civilised nations" invoked at Versailles — the standard against which societies were measured for international recognition and self-governance — was an explicitly Christian secular construct. Bharat's entry into the modern international order was thus conditioned on its acceptance of a framework whose civilisational presuppositions were alien to its own traditions.

Sai Deepak is also direct about the differential treatment of Hindu religious institutions under the constitutional framework: while Bharat's Constitution guarantees minorities — including religious minorities — extensive rights to manage their own institutions, Hindu temples and endowments remain under State control through a web of legislative acts in state after state, a differential that is, on any principled reading, a colonial inheritance dressed in democratic clothing.

Significance

Why This Book Matters Now

In the decades since independence, the dominant intellectual frameworks for understanding Bharat's condition have come either from Western liberalism, Marxist historiography, or Nehruvian nationalism — all of which, Sai Deepak argues, are themselves products of the same European colonial epistemology they claim to critique. India, that is Bharat represents a substantive attempt to step outside this closed circle.

What distinguishes the book from much civilisational advocacy writing is its method. Sai Deepak is a Supreme Court advocate, and his argument is not merely rhetorical but evidentiary — marshalled from charters, legislative debates, archival records, and the comparative decolonial literature. This combination of civilisational vision and juridical precision is rare, and it is what makes the book a genuine intellectual contribution rather than polemics dressed as scholarship.

Just a century ago, our scholars and philosophers who were rooted in the Sanatana ethos applied just one word to describe the underlying nature of European colonialism: Asuric. This is the exact vocabulary that we must reclaim today if we are to fully reclaim the precolonial narrative of India.

— Sandeep Balakrishna, Scholar & Author of Invaders and Infidels

The book ends with an appeal to Bharat's public institutions — its judiciary, its executive, its intellectual class — to engage seriously with the indigenous traditions from a decolonised standpoint. It does not prescribe a return to any pre-colonial utopia. It argues, rather, that genuine self-governance requires a genuine self-understanding: that India can only be truly free when it stops seeing itself through its coloniser's eyes.

As the title itself signals — drawn word for word from Article 1 of the Constitution — the country's founding charter already contains within it the seed of this recovery. Bharat is not an afterthought. It is there from the very first sentence, waiting to be heard.
