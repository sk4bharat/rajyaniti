Why Bharat Cannot Be
Reduced to a Single Narrative

Every attempt to define India in one sentence collapses under the weight of its diversity. Perhaps the plurality is the point — and always was.

12 min read Long-form essay
Twitter Copy Link

The search for a unified theory of India has occupied philosophers, administrators, and nationalists for centuries. Each time someone thinks they have found the formula, the subcontinent offers a quiet, devastating counter-example.

India is a riddle that rewards not resolution but attentiveness. To hold it in your mind without forcing a conclusion is itself a discipline — one that the civilization has been practising for millennia, long before it became a political necessity.

Across the hills of Meghalaya and the deserts of Rajasthan, across the fishing communities of Kerala and the wheat-farmers of Haryana, something persists — not a shared story exactly, but a shared willingness to coexist inside contradiction. This willingness is older than the nation-state. It may, in fact, be the only thing that makes the nation-state possible.

I. The Error of the Single Story

The philosopher Ramchandra Gandhi once observed that any honest encounter with India begins in bewilderment. Not the bewilderment of ignorance, but the bewilderment of surplus — too many truths, too many histories, too many gods, too many silences. The temptation, understandably, is to resolve this surplus into a single manageable narrative. The colonial administration tried. The postcolonial state tried. Social media tries every day.

None of these projects have succeeded. What they have produced instead is a series of useful half-truths: India as spiritual civilization, India as emerging economy, India as wounded civilizational state seeking redress. Each of these frames illuminates something real. Each also casts a long shadow over what it cannot see.

Mythology is not primitive science. It is civilisational memory — encoded in story because story outlasts empires.

II. What Plurality Costs

To say that plurality is India's defining feature is not the same as celebrating it uncritically. Plurality has a price. It can mean that injustice hides behind custom, that violence finds shelter in difference, that accountability becomes impossible because no single authority can be held responsible for the whole. The caste system is not external to this pluralism — it is, in many respects, its dark shadow.

And yet. To abandon plurality in favour of enforced unity is not the cure. History offers a fairly clear verdict on what happens when a civilization as vast and differentiated as this one is pressed into a single mould: it breaks, or it pretends, or it turns violent. Sometimes all three, in sequence.

The Grammar of Civilisational Depth

What the Vedic, the Buddhist, the Jain, the Sufi, the Bhakti, and the Dalit traditions share is not a common theology. They share a common refusal: the refusal to accept that the world is simpler than it appears. Each tradition, in its own idiom, has insisted on complexity, on the irreducibility of experience, on the inadequacy of any single frame. This is not a minor philosophical coincidence. It is the grammar of a civilization.

III. The Contemporary Stakes

These questions are not merely academic. In the India of 2026, the question of what the country is — or should be — is bitterly contested. The contest is not just political. It reaches into language, memory, architecture, food, education, and the stories parents choose to tell their children at bedtime.

To reduce this contest to a binary — tradition versus modernity, Hindutva versus secularism, centre versus margin — is to repeat the error of the single narrative at a smaller scale. The reality is more uncomfortable: there are twenty serious arguments happening simultaneously, and the most honest position is one that can hold several of them in view without pretending to adjudicate from outside.

IV. What Comes After Bewilderment

Bewilderment, if sustained long enough, becomes something useful: curiosity. And curiosity, if pursued honestly, produces the one thing that the single narrative can never offer — a real encounter with the thing itself, in its fullness, on its own terms.

The writers, historians, and thinkers who have understood India best — Tagore, Ambedkar, Nehru at his most lucid, Ramanujan, Thapar, Dhareshwar — have all shared a willingness to be surprised. None of them arrived with the answer. All of them left with better questions.

Perhaps that is what Bharat asks of those who would understand it: not resolution, but attention. Not a theory, but a practice. Not a single story, but the capacity to listen — really listen — to many stories at once, without collapsing them into one.
