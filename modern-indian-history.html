The Manufactured Crisis:
Sati Pratha and the Colonial Lie

How British Evangelicals and Baptist missionaries weaponised a rare, exceptional act into a systemic indictment of Hindu civilisation — and why the numbers never added up.

18 min read · May 2026 · Colonial Era · 1790–1829 · Based on Dr. Meenakshi Jain
SK
Sandeep Kumar sk4bharat Editorial

The story of Sati Pratha as it has been handed down through popular imagination — thousands of women forced onto funeral pyres every year, a barbaric custom endemic to Hindu society, rescued from itself only by enlightened British legislation — is, upon careful historical examination, largely a fabrication. It was constructed over three deliberate decades by a coalition of Evangelical Christians and Baptist missionaries who needed a moral horror story to justify their ambitions in India. The scholarship of Dr. Meenakshi Jain, culminating in her meticulously documented 2016 work Sati: Evangelicals, Baptist Missionaries, and the Changing Colonial Discourse, has dismantled this narrative with the cold precision that primary sources demand.

This is not a comfortable reassessment. It requires us to revisit not only a colonial-era policy debate, but the very mechanisms by which atrocity literature is manufactured — how numbers are conjured, how testimony is ventriloquised, and how a self-serving political agenda is dressed in the language of humanitarian concern. As Jain demonstrates, the template was not new when it was deployed against Hindu society. But it was perhaps never applied more thoroughly.

I. Before the Storm: The Orientalist Compact

The early phase of British India was not characterised by contempt for Hindu customs. Under the influence of Orientalist administrators — most prominently Warren Hastings, the first Governor-General — official policy was one of studied respect for Indian law, religion, and tradition. British officials were instructed to understand and work within the existing social and legal frameworks of the subcontinent. Sati, in this era, was observed with curiosity and sometimes unease, but not with the moral outrage that would define the next generation's discourse.

Foreign travellers who documented India during this period recorded sati as a rarity — a striking spectacle precisely because it was unusual. Jain traces accounts from the Greeks (recorded as far back as Alexander's campaign in 326 BC) through medieval Arab and European visitors, all of whom remarked on sati as an exceptional act. By the seventeenth century, it had become something of a required set piece in travellers' accounts of India — a wonder to be noted, perhaps invented or embellished, because audiences expected it. The demand for the exotic, Jain observes, drove a supply of testimony that was not always grounded in direct witness.

Sati was an "exceptional act," performed by a miniscule number of Hindu widows over the centuries. Its occurrence was, however, exaggerated in the nineteenth century by Evangelicals and Baptist missionaries eager to Anglicise and Christianise India.

— Dr. Meenakshi Jain, Sati: Evangelicals, Baptist Missionaries, and the Changing Colonial Discourse (2016)

What shattered this relative equilibrium was the arrival of a new ideological force in British India: evangelical Christianity. Its trigger was Charles Grant, an employee of the East India Company and later a member of its Court of Directors. In 1792, Grant presented his influential treatise Observations on the State of Society among the Asiatic Subjects of Great Britain — a document that challenged the Orientalist policy at its roots. Grant argued that introducing the language and religion of the conquerors would most effectively assimilate the conquered. His was the first major public argument that India needed not to be governed on its own terms, but reformed on British ones.

II. Manufacturing the Campaign: 1803–1829

Jain divides the evangelical-missionary campaign against sati into two phases. Between 1803 and 1813, the case was prepared — the intellectual and moral framework assembled, the British public's sympathies cultivated. From 1813 to 1829, the campaign entered its operational phase: figures were produced to validate claims. This chronological distinction is crucial, because it reveals the campaign's deliberately staged character.

The Baptist missionaries who had established themselves in Serampore (then a Danish enclave near Calcutta, outside East India Company jurisdiction) were the primary architects. William Carey, Joshua Marshman, and William Ward built an extraordinary propaganda operation. Ward, in particular, was a prolific and creative statistician. Jain documents how Ward calculated — with elaborate but ultimately imagined breakdowns — that 10,500 people were sacrificed annually to Hindu gods. Then, on the very next page of his own publication, he doubled his estimate of annual sati cases from five thousand to ten thousand. The adjustment required no new data. It required only a more urgent narrative.

The Numbers Game — Missionary Estimates vs. Reality

Missionary and Evangelical figures for annual sati deaths ranged wildly, shaped by rhetorical necessity rather than evidence. Rev. David Brown, citing William Chambers, estimated 50,000 annual cases. Charles Grant hypothesised 33,000. William Ward published 10,000. When the British government finally began keeping its own registry in 1815, the actual numbers for all three Presidencies over ten years came to 6,632 — a cumulative figure, not an annual one.

The missionaries were not simply lying in a crude sense. They operated within a framework of evangelical conviction that permitted them to treat moral urgency as sufficient grounds for statistical assertion. If the evil was real, then the numbers would confirm it — and if the numbers did not yet exist, they could be projected from the known wickedness of idolatry. The circularity of the argument was invisible to those who already believed its premise.

The Bengal Anomaly

When the British government began formally registering sati cases in 1815 — a process that continued until 1828 — the data it collected produced an immediate contradiction. In the ten years between 1815 and 1824, 6,632 cases were reported across the three Presidencies of Bengal, Bombay, and Madras. Of these, an astonishing 5,997 — fully 90.4 per cent — were recorded in Bengal.

This distribution was geographically paradoxical. Bengal had not been historically associated with sati as a widespread practice. Madras and Bombay — regions with deeper, older Hindu traditions of widow observance — contributed negligible numbers. The Judge of Malabar notified authorities that the practice was entirely absent in his area. The Judge of Trichinopoly reported that he could trace no instance of widow immolation in the previous ten years in his district. Meanwhile, Bengal — where Baptist missionaries had concentrated precisely since 1799 — contributed nine in every ten cases.

Jain raises the pointed question: was the high incidence of sati in Bengal a missionary manufacture? The geographical clustering of cases precisely where missionary influence was strongest, and their near-total absence everywhere else, is not easily explained by neutral factors. As the distinguished historian Christopher Bayly observed, the government's own figures revealed just how limited the actual number of satis were — between 1817 and 1827, only 4,323 cases were reported for a population of some 160 million across British India.

Thousands of pages of parliamentary papers dealt with 4,000 immolations, while the death of millions from famine and starvation was mentioned only incidentally — sometimes only because it tended indirectly to increase the number of widows performing the practice.

— Christopher Bayly, cited in Dr. Meenakshi Jain, Sati (2016)

III. The Demography the Missionaries Ignored

The missionary campaign was built on a particular image of sati: young women, coerced or compelled by family or caste, thrown onto pyres against their will. This image served the narrative of victimhood that could mobilise British public opinion, particularly the newly politicised women of the evangelical movement. Jain notes that sati was the first political issue in British India in which British women were directly involved — mobilising support for their allegedly helpless Indian sisters.

But the British government's own registered data flatly contradicted this portrait. Government statistics showed that almost half of all sati cases involved women in the age group of fifty and above; two-thirds were forty or older. Less than five per cent of all recorded cases between 1815 and 1829 involved women between the ages of eleven and twenty. The typical person performing sati, according to the British government's own data, was an elderly widow — not the young bride of missionary pamphlets.

Furthermore, the first-hand accounts of British officers who witnessed sati — the very officers who had to enforce attempts at dissuasion — told a more complex story than the missionary literature acknowledged. Many officers spoke to the women directly; most who were spoken to stated plainly that if not permitted to die with their husbands, they would starve themselves to death. Jain notes that officers frequently recorded how families themselves — far from coercing — often actively tried to persuade the woman not to proceed, reminding her of her responsibilities to children or other dependants. Once decided, however, such women rarely relented.

IV. Indophobia by Design — From Indomania to Indictment

To understand how the climate shifted so dramatically between the Orientalist era and the missionary era, Jain traces an intellectual transformation she characterises as the conversion of British Indomania into Indophobia. Early British scholars had celebrated Indian civilisation; Sanskrit grammars, Hindu philosophy, Mughal administrative sophistication all attracted genuine admiration. Warren Hastings had been a patron of this Orientalism. The missionaries and the utilitarian philosophers who allied with them — most damagingly, James Mill in his 1817 History of British India — set about dismantling it systematically.

Mill had never visited India. He did not read Sanskrit, Persian, or any Indian language. Yet his History became the standard text for East India Company officials and eventually a set text for candidates to the Indian Civil Service. In it, Mill characterised Hindu civilisation as representing "the rudest and weakest state of the human mind" and declared that Hindus were "perfectly destitute of historical records." These sweeping condemnations — produced from a London study chair — shaped the assumptions of an entire generation of administrators who would go on to implement policy in India.

Sati was the perfect vehicle for this project. It was dramatic, visually horrifying, capable of being stripped of context and presented as the self-evident proof of Hindu barbarism. Parliamentary papers devoted thousands of pages to a practice that affected, at its very most, a few hundred families a year — while the Bengal famine, which killed millions, was mentioned only as a statistical footnote, noted occasionally because it produced more widows and thus, in the missionary calculus, more potential sati cases.

V. The Politics of Lord Bentinck's Minute

The legislation that ended sati — Lord William Bentinck's Regulation XVII of 1829 — is celebrated as a humanitarian triumph in the colonial historiography that subsequent generations absorbed. Bentinck himself, however, was more candid than that historiography. His own Minute on the subject, preserved in full by Jain, is instructive in what it reveals about the actual calculations at play.

Bentinck wrote that had sati been prevalent in the upper provinces — present-day Uttar Pradesh and parts of Bihar — from which most of the Company's Indian soldiers were drawn, he would have been considerably more circumspect. The people there, he noted, were more "bold and manly." The legislation, in other words, was calibrated not purely on moral grounds, but on an assessment of which populations could be subjected to social engineering without military risk. Bengal's relative political passivity made it a safe terrain for coercive reform.

This calculation sits uncomfortably with the humanitarian narrative. It suggests that the timing and scope of the ban was shaped as much by imperial risk-management as by moral imperative. Jain does not suggest that sati was admirable or that its prohibition was wrong in its outcome. Her point is more precise and more damaging: the justification offered for the intervention was built on systematically distorted data, deployed within a political project whose real aims were Anglicisation and Christianisation — not the welfare of Indian women.

Lord Bentinck's Regulation XVII of 1829 marked the culmination of a sustained campaign against Hinduism by British Evangelicals and missionaries anxious to Anglicise and Christianise India.

— Dr. Meenakshi Jain, Sati: Evangelicals, Baptist Missionaries, and the Changing Colonial Discourse (2016)

VI. The Atrocity Literature Template

Jain's book has been described by reviewers as documenting the birth of atrocity literature and its first systematic application in India. The template she identifies is familiar to historians of propaganda: identify a real but marginal practice; inflate its frequency through selective data collection concentrated in missionary-controlled zones; generate emotional testimony that bypasses statistical scrutiny; mobilise domestic audiences through appeals to gender solidarity; and finally, use the resulting moral outrage to justify an intervention that serves entirely separate strategic goals.

The template was not invented for India. Evangelical campaigns in Britain had employed similar methods in debates over slavery. But India offered a uniquely fertile terrain: a civilisation sufficiently foreign to British audiences that claims about it were difficult to verify, a press hungry for sensational material, and a commercial interest — the East India Company — that had its own reasons to welcome a moral narrative justifying continued and expanded presence.

One detail from Jain's research has a particular contemporary resonance. The missionaries who were most aggressively inflating sati numbers were simultaneously very quiet about the practice of witch-burning in European and American history — a tradition that had resulted in the deaths of tens of thousands of women, many of them elderly, over two centuries of active persecution. The selective outrage was not lost on later commentators. A civilisation capable of burning women as witches was, by the nineteenth century, positioning itself as the moral guardian of Indian women from their own families. The hypocrisy was structural, not accidental.

VII. What the Scholarship Demands of Us

Dr. Meenakshi Jain is a former Fellow of the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library and has served as Associate Professor of History at Gargi College, University of Delhi. She was awarded the Padma Shri in 2020. Her method is consistently that of the trained historian: primary sources, primary sources, primary sources. She does not speculate where documents can speak. Her treatment of the British government's own registry data — turning the colonial state's numbers against the colonial state's narrative — is a particular model of historiographical precision.

What her work demands of subsequent readers and scholars is not a reflexive reversal — an insistence that sati was acceptable or that its abolition was a mistake. It demands something more intellectually rigorous: a willingness to distinguish between the act and the story told about the act, between a social reality and the political uses to which that reality was put. It demands that we ask, as Jain does throughout, who benefited from the narrative, who constructed the data, and what was not said.

The broader implications extend well beyond sati. The methods Jain documents — exaggerated statistics, geographically concentrated reporting, emotionally overwhelming individual anecdotes, the systematic marginalisation of Indian voices in favour of missionary testimony — recur across colonial discourse on India. The indictment of Hindu society that was built around sati became the template for indictments of caste, of temple worship, of indigenous education, of the entire fabric of civilisational life. Each campaign followed the same rhythm: prepare the moral case, produce figures to validate it, legislate on the basis of those figures, and appoint the legislators as saviours.

Meenakshi Jain's work stands as a reminder that the past is not simply recovered — it is contested, reconstructed, and defended. The false narrative of sati pratha was not merely an error. It was a project. Naming it as such is not revisionism. It is history doing its actual work.

Further Reading — Primary Source

Sati: Evangelicals, Baptist Missionaries, and the Changing Colonial Discourse by Dr. Meenakshi Jain (Aryan Books International, 2016) includes the full text of Lord Bentinck's Minute on Sati (1829), the relevant colonial legislation, and an extensive assembly of primary-source accounts spanning from Greek records of 326 BC to missionary publications of the 1820s. It is an indispensable reference for any serious engagement with this history.
